The Fee Business Behind the Vault Door

Wilmington Trust lists royalty escrow as a named service. Its M&A team administers 24,000 payments a year worth $29 billion. Escrow is not a product. It is a fee business.

By Prime Domain Capital Research · November 18, 2026 · 6-minute read

On a closing day in Wilmington, Delaware, an escrow officer signs off on a wire that is not hers to keep. The money belongs to the sellers of a company that was just sold; it will sit in an account at the bank for eighteen months, and it will be released in pieces — some to the sellers, some to the buyer, some to nobody, if the indemnification claims never arrive. The bank earns a fee for holding it. The fee is small next to the sum held. The sum held is large, and the bank holds a great many of them at once.

That is the escrow business, and royalty escrow is its quietest corner. Wilmington Trust — one of the largest institutional trust companies in the United States, a subsidiary of M&T Bank, reported at $213.5 billion in assets and $84.2 billion under management — lists royalty escrow as a named service on its business escrow page, alongside escrow for mergers and acquisitions, litigation settlements, and bankruptcies. The description is plain: royalty payments, from intellectual property such as music, books, artwork, or patents, held by a neutral third party until the rightful recipient is established. The bank is not selling a new idea. It is selling a service it already performs, under a name its clients already use.

The M&A side of the same desk shows the scale of the machinery. Wilmington Trust's M&A team services 3,200 M&A accounts and administers 24,000 M&A payments a year, valued at $29 billion. The pattern is the same across the industry: in a private-target acquisition, the buyer typically holds back 10 to 20 percent of the purchase price in escrow against indemnification claims, and SRS Acquiom — which has administered more than 10,000 deals worth over $1.7 trillion since 2007 — found in its 2026 Earnouts & Claims Study of 1,174 private-target acquisitions that nearly three in ten deals experience an indemnification claim. The escrow account is where the claim is paid from, or where the money is released from when no claim arrives. Either way, the administrator is paid for the holding.

Why the economics work

Escrow is an unusual business because the capital is somebody else's. The bank does not lend the money in the account; it holds it, reconciles it, and releases it on instruction. The risk is operational — a misdirected wire, a missed deadline, a release to the wrong party — rather than credit risk, and the fee is charged for custody, administration, and the reputation that the money will still be there when the conditions are met. Research firms size the market accordingly: the escrow accounts market was estimated at $31.4 billion in 2024 and projected to reach $68.2 billion by 2033, a 9.2 percent compound annual rate, according to Growth Market Reports; escrow-as-a-service — the software layer that lets smaller firms offer the same holding — was estimated at $5.0 billion in 2024 and projected to reach $15.2 billion by 2033, a 12.3 percent rate, according to DataHorizzon Research. Source-code escrow, the narrowest corner, was estimated at $2.48 billion in 2024. These are third-party estimates and they disagree with each other on scope, as such estimates do — but they agree on direction, and the direction is growth.

Royalty escrow fits the same economics with one advantage the M&A business lacks: recurrence. A deal escrow lasts eighteen months and ends. A royalty escrow renews every month, for as long as the well produces or the song is streamed. Federal oil and gas royalties alone generated more than $14 billion in fiscal 2024, and the Office of Natural Resources Revenue disbursed $16.45 billion that year. Global recorded music revenue was $29.6 billion in 2024, and The Mechanical Licensing Collective was holding about $519.8 million in blanket royalties pending distribution at the end of that year. Every dollar of it passes through a holding account before it reaches its owner, and every holding account pays a fee to whoever administers it.

The other side of the argument

The counterargument is that escrow fees are thin, the work is grinding, and the business is harder than it looks. Custody fees are measured in basis points, and the reconciliation work — division orders in oil and gas, work registrations in music, cap tables in M&A — is labor-intensive and unforgiving of error. A single misdirected disbursement can cost more than years of fees on the account, and the trust business is reputation-driven: one failure ends the relationship, and the relationship is the asset. Fintech entrants are compressing margins further, productizing the holding layer as software and charging less for it. And the largest trust banks may not want a new brand at all — Wilmington Trust already has one, and a domain that describes the service is more useful to a challenger than to the incumbent.

That is a fair description of the business, and it points to the buyer rather than away from it. The challengers — the royalty administrators, the fintech escrow platforms, the regional trust companies building a national practice — are the ones who need a name that states the service, because they cannot rely on a century of brand equity to explain what they do. The incumbents sell trust. The challengers sell a service, and the service has a name. The fee pool grows with deal volume and royalty volume alike, and the firms that administer the holding are doing the same job in every industry that pays royalties. The name that describes that job belongs to whoever claims it first.

What follows

Three things are worth watching. First, whether escrow-as-a-service keeps growing at the double-digit rate the research firms project — if it does, the holding layer becomes a software product, and the product needs a category name. Second, whether the royalty administrators consolidate: the firms that hold disputed oil proceeds, match music royalties, and administer M&A holdbacks are doing one job under three labels, and consolidation usually produces a single brand. Third, whether a trust bank or a fintech firm claims the royalty escrow category outright — because the phrase is already a named service on a major trust bank's website, and the firm that owns the exact name owns the search, the pitch, and the first call.
