The Escrow Layer Behind Every Stream

Spotify paid $10 billion to the music industry in 2024. Half a billion dollars of songwriting royalties sat unmatched at the end of that year. Somebody holds the money in between.

By Prime Domain Capital Research · November 18, 2026 · 6-minute read

A songwriter in Nashville opens her royalty statement on a Tuesday morning and finds a line she cannot explain: a few hundred dollars from a song she co-wrote four years ago, paid by a service she has never used, through a publisher she has never met. The money is real. The path it took is not on the statement. Somewhere between the listener who pressed play and the writer who is owed the money, the payment passed through three organizations, a matching database, and a holding account — and at each step, somebody decided when the money moved and when it waited.

The scale of that waiting is the story. Global recorded music revenue reached $29.6 billion in 2024, up 4.8 percent for the tenth consecutive year of growth, according to the IFPI's Global Music Report 2025. Streaming accounted for $20.4 billion of it — 69.0 percent of the total, the first time streaming passed $20 billion — with 752 million paid subscription users, up 10.6 percent. In the United States alone, recorded music revenue was $17.7 billion at retail in 2024, with streaming at $14.9 billion, or 84 percent of the total, and paid subscriptions crossing 100 million for the first time, according to the RIAA. Spotify, the largest single payer, paid $10 billion to the music industry in 2024 — the largest annual payment by any single retailer — and has paid out roughly $60 billion over its lifetime, with about 70 percent of its music revenue going back to the industry.

That money does not arrive as a single check. It arrives as millions of micro-payments that must be matched to the right works and the right owners, and the matching is where the escrow layer lives. The Mechanical Licensing Collective, created by the Music Modernization Act to collect and distribute mechanical royalties from streaming services in the United States, processed $1.05 billion in 2024 distributions and directly distributed $814.7 million. At the end of 2024 it was holding about $519.8 million in blanket royalties pending distribution — roughly $273.8 million unmatched, or 8.4 percent of the pools, and about $225.2 million unclaimed, or 6.9 percent. Its average initial match rate in 2024 was 84.1 percent. The other 15.9 percent is money waiting: for a songwriter to register, for a publisher to claim a share, for a database to catch up with a catalog.

The institutions that hold the money

The MLC is the newest holder, not the only one. ASCAP, the performance rights organization, reported record 2024 revenue of $1.835 billion, up 5.7 percent, with $1.696 billion available for royalty distributions — up 6.5 percent — and an overhead rate of 10 percent, meaning 90 cents of every dollar reached members. BMI, its rival, reported revenue of $1.573 billion and distributions of $1.471 billion, and was acquired by the private equity firm New Mountain Capital in 2024 — a transaction that valued a royalty-collection business the way a buyer values any cash-flow business. SoundExchange, which collects digital performance royalties for recordings, passed $12 billion in cumulative distributions since 2003 in February 2025, with 2024 gross distributions of $1.05 billion, up 4.9 percent over 2023, and more than 90 percent of its collections distributed within 45 days at an administrative rate of about 4.5 percent. Each of these organizations is, in part, an escrow operation: money comes in, sits while ownership is established, and goes out when the match is made.

The holding is not a defect in the system. It is the system. Streaming pays in aggregate and in arrears; a song streamed in January is reported, matched, and paid months later. Works change hands, co-writers fall out, publishers merge, and catalogs are sold with incomplete paperwork. The MLC's database held more than 44.4 million works at the end of 2024, with 11 million new registrations in the year — a volume at which even a 16 percent unmatched rate is a rounding error in percentage terms and half a billion dollars in cash terms. The money that sits unmatched is not lost; it is held, and the holding itself is a service with a fee attached.

The other side of the argument

The counterargument is that the escrow layer is shrinking, not growing, and that framing it as a problem overstates the case. SoundExchange distributes more than 90 percent of collections within 45 days. ASCAP passes 90 cents of every dollar to members. The MLC's match rate is 84.1 percent and rising, and its total distributions since January 2021 passed $4.2 billion by September 2026. Against $29.6 billion in global recorded music revenue, the $519.8 million held by the MLC is under 2 percent — a small, shrinking residue, not a crisis. And the MLC's own rule for the residue is defensible: beginning in April 2027, remaining unmatched and unclaimed royalties will be distributed by market share, which at least puts the money to work instead of holding it indefinitely.

That defense is mostly right, and it still concedes the point that matters. Market-share distribution is a transfer: money that could not be matched to its owner goes to the largest publishers instead. The small songwriter who never registered loses twice — once when the money is held, and again when it is released to somebody else. The 84.1 percent match rate is a triumph of administration and a reminder that the remaining 15.9 percent is a standing pool of hundreds of millions of dollars, renewed every year by new releases, new co-writes, and new catalog sales. Somebody will administer that pool for the next decade. The fee for doing so is real, recurring, and attached to a service every participant in the chain already understands.

What follows

Three things are worth watching. First, the April 2027 market-share distributions — the first time the MLC releases its unmatched pool at scale, and the moment the escrow question becomes a distribution question with winners and losers. Second, whether the match rate keeps climbing as the database grows, or whether the volume of new works outruns the matching and the held pool grows again. Third, whether the royalty platforms that hold the money start to look like trust companies — because the job of holding, matching, and releasing other people's royalties is the same job a trust bank does for oil and gas proceeds, and the firms that do it best will want a name that says what they do.
