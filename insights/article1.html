The Money Nobody Can Touch

When ownership of a well is disputed, the checks stop — and Wyoming law says the money must sit in escrow. The operators who ignore that rule pay 18 percent a year.

By Prime Domain Capital Research · November 18, 2026 · 6-minute read

The letter arrives in Casper in an envelope with an operator's return address, and it says less than the recipient hoped. The well under her family's land produced all year. The oil was sold. Her share of the proceeds exists — the operator's own records say so — but the check is not in the envelope, because two cousins are arguing over who inherited the mineral interest, and while they argue, the operator is holding the money. Not in escrow. Just holding it.

That distinction is the whole subject of this article. In Wyoming, holding it is not an option the law leaves open. The Royalty Payment Act requires an operator that withholds production proceeds because ownership is disputed to place the money in escrow — a neutral account, out of the operator's hands, where it waits until the dispute is settled. The statute also sets a clock: proceeds from a first sale must be paid no later than six months after the first day of the month following that sale, and proceeds from later sales no later than 60 days after the end of the calendar month in which the sale occurred. Miss the clock, and the operator owes the unpaid amount plus 18 percent annual interest. That rate is not an accident. It is set above the market on purpose, so that holding somebody else's money never pays.

The Wyoming Supreme Court said so again in 2025. In Chesapeake Exploration v. Morton Production Co., the court affirmed a Royalty Payment Act violation where disputed production proceeds were withheld without being placed in escrow as the statute requires. The case is the latest in a line that runs back to Independent Producers Marketing Corp. v. Cobb in 1986, where the court described the statute's purpose in a sentence that has done more work than most statutes: to stop oil producers from retaining other people's money for their own use. A decade later, in Wolter v. Equitable Resources, the record showed an operator that had begun depositing overriding royalty payments into an escrow account in January 1995 — the mechanism working exactly as designed, money parked with a third party while the parties fought over who owned it.

The scale of the money in transit

Escrow rules sound like a corner of oil and gas law until the volumes are put beside them. The United States produced a record 13.6 million barrels of crude oil a day in 2025, up 3 percent from the previous record of 13.2 million in 2024, according to the Energy Information Administration — and the Permian Basin alone produced about 6.6 million barrels a day, roughly 48 percent of the national total. Federal oil and gas royalties generated more than $14 billion in revenue in fiscal 2024, and companies paid roughly $96 billion in royalties over fiscal 2014–2024, according to the Government Accountability Office. The Office of Natural Resources Revenue disbursed $16.45 billion in fiscal 2024 — $4.29 billion to 33 states, $1.18 billion to Tribes and individual Indian mineral owners — the fourth-largest annual disbursement since 1982, and more than $387 billion has been disbursed since then.

Those are federal numbers, and Wyoming's statute governs state and private wells, but the point carries across: royalty money is measured in billions, it moves through a small number of hands, and every dispute over who owns a share creates a sum that must sit somewhere. The royalty rate itself is modest — 12.5 percent onshore, the statutory floor under the Mineral Leasing Act of 1920, and 12.5 to 18.75 percent offshore depending on lease vintage — but the rate applies to production measured in millions of barrels a day, so even a single disputed well can hold six figures in escrow while lawyers argue over a deed.

Why the escrow rule exists

The rule exists because the alternative is profitable. An operator that withholds disputed proceeds keeps the cash, earns the return on it, and pays it out — if ever — only when a court forces the issue. The 18 percent interest rate and the escrow mandate together remove that profit: the money leaves the operator's account either way, and the interest makes delay more expensive than settlement. The GAO's 2025 report shows how slow the underlying accounting can be — net adjustments to federal royalty payments over fiscal 2014–2024 ran to negative $2.7 billion, about 2.8 percent of what was paid, and $300 million of adjustments reached back to payments made four to six years earlier, with the Office of Natural Resources Revenue approving $352 million in refunds and holding up to seven years to verify accuracy. When the accounting itself runs years behind the production, a rule that parks disputed money with a neutral party is the only thing standing between a mineral owner and an operator's balance sheet.

The other side of the argument

Operators have a case, and it deserves a hearing. Title disputes can take years to resolve, and escrowing the proceeds ties up capital that could be deployed elsewhere — a real cost for a small operator with a thin balance sheet. The 18 percent rate, set when interest rates were far higher, can look punitive when the delay comes from a county clerk's missing record rather than bad faith. And escrow does not resolve the dispute; it only parks the money, so the mineral owner still waits, just with the comfort that the money is not earning returns for the other side. There is also a quieter problem the statute does not solve: many royalty owners never learn their proceeds were withheld at all. The rule protects the owners who know enough to sue. The ones who do not know are still waiting on a check that was never sent, in a system where the accounting runs years behind.

That is the honest limit of the escrow rule — and it is also the reason the service behind it is a business rather than a legal footnote. Somebody has to hold the money, reconcile the division orders, track the heirs, and release the funds when the title clears. The statute creates the obligation. The obligation creates the account. The account needs an administrator, and the administrator needs a name that says what it does before the first call. A domain that states the service in two words is the front door to a market the law keeps open by force.

What follows

Three things are worth watching. First, whether other producing states copy Wyoming's model — the 2025 Chesapeake decision gives mineral owners in every state a fresh citation, and legislatures tend to borrow statutes that courts have just enforced. Second, whether the royalty accounting gap the GAO documented narrows or widens: $300 million in adjustments to payments four to six years old is a measure of how much money sits in the wrong hands, and every dollar of it is a candidate for an escrow account. Third, whether the administrators who do this work consolidate — because the firms that hold disputed proceeds, reconcile division orders, and pay heirs are doing the same job in every basin, and the name that describes that job belongs to whoever claims it first.
