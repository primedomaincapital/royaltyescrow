What Royalty Escrow Costs: Fees, Minimums, and the Price of a Dispute

No royalty escrow publishes a fee schedule. Setup runs $1,500 to $5,000, administration 0.25% to 1% a year — and a contested royalty costs 18% a year in Wyoming before the bank takes a dollar.

By Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026 · 9-minute read

The operator's landman called it a routine matter. A well in the Powder River Basin had produced for two years, the checks had gone out on time, and then a second heir appeared with a deed the first heir had never seen. The operator stopped the payments the same week — the safe move, the lawyers said — and the money went into an escrow account at a Wyoming bank, where it would sit until a court or an agreement sorted out who owned the interest. Nobody on the call asked what the escrow would cost. That was the mistake. By the time the title cleared, the account had been open for nineteen months, and the cost of holding the money had become a second dispute, layered on top of the first.

Royalty escrow is priced like a service nobody shops for until they need it, which is the worst time to learn the price. There is no published fee schedule for royalty escrow. No operator, bank, or administrator posts one. The cost is negotiated in the escrow agreement, charged against the funds in the account, and discovered — usually by the royalty owner — only when the money is finally released and the statement shows what was taken out. What follows is the closest thing to a price list the market allows: the statutory costs, the comparable escrow markets, and the dispute charges that turn a holding account into an expensive one.

The setup: what it costs to open the account

Start with what the law requires, because in Wyoming the law writes the first lines of the invoice. When payment cannot be made within the statutory time, the operator must deposit all proceeds credited to the eventual owner into an escrow account in a federally insured bank or savings and loan in Wyoming, using a standard escrow document form approved by the Attorney General, and the deposit must earn interest at the highest rate the institution offers for the amount and term. The account is not a ledger entry on the operator's books. It is a real account, at a real bank, under a state-approved form — and the bank charges for holding it.

How much the bank charges is the first number nobody publishes. The closest public benchmarks come from adjacent escrow markets. Professional escrow agents in commercial transactions charge setup fees ranging from $1,500 to $5,000, with annual administration fees between 0.25 percent and 1.0 percent of average assets under management, according to a 2026 survey of cross-border escrow terms — a useful anchor, not a royalty quote, because royalty escrow is usually opened by an operator under a statutory duty rather than negotiated between two commercial parties. In the merger market, where escrow is a negotiated line item, a bank trust department is the usual agent: Wilmington Trust services approximately 3,700 escrow engagements a year, and the J.P. Morgan 2025 M&A Holdback Escrow Study, which analyzed more than 2,400 transactions over three years, treats the escrow agent as a priced service rather than a courtesy. The royalty owner who assumes the account is free is assuming the bank works for nothing.

The annual administration is the line that compounds. On a $100,000 suspense balance, 0.25 percent is $250 a year and 1.0 percent is $1,000 — small numbers against the balance, and large ones against a royalty owner whose monthly check was $400. The fee is typically charged against the escrowed funds themselves, which means the owner pays it out of money that was already theirs, for the privilege of having it held from them. Add wire fees, document preparation, and the per-disbursement charges that most agents add, and a small suspense balance can shrink by hundreds of dollars a year before a single dollar reaches the owner.

The minimums: when the check never comes at all

The second cost is not a fee but a threshold. Most operators pay only when the accumulated balance reaches a minimum, and the minimum is set in the division order — the document the royalty owner signs that states the fractional interest, the type of interest, and the certification of title. In Texas, the statutory division-order terms allow proceeds of less than $100 to be accrued and held until the total reaches $100 or twelve months pass, whichever comes first, and amounts under $10 may be held until production ceases altogether. One operator, Vitruvian Exploration, sets its minimum pay amount at $25, with amounts below it paid annually in September. The effect is the same: a small interest does not get a monthly check. It gets an annual one, or none, and the owner who calls to ask where the money is learns that the money is there — it is simply below the line.

The minimum interacts with suspension in a way that catches heirs off guard. A suspended interest accrues, but the accrual does not trigger payment until the suspension is cured and the balance clears the minimum. An heir who inherits a sixteenth of a royalty interest in a marginal well can wait a year for a check that totals less than the cost of the certified mail that announced the suspension. The system is not designed to be cruel. It is designed for the operator's accounting convenience, and the convenience is paid for by the smallest owners.

The dispute: where the real money goes

The fee schedule that applies when a royalty is contested is not written by the bank. It is written by the statutes, and it is the most expensive part of the story. In Wyoming, an operator that violates the Royalty Payment Act is liable for the unpaid proceeds plus interest at 18 percent a year from the due date — a rate set deliberately above the market, so that holding somebody else's money never pays. The Wyoming Supreme Court said so again in 2025, in Chesapeake Exploration v. Morton Production Co., affirming a violation where disputed proceeds were withheld without being placed in escrow as the statute requires. The court has been saying it since 1986, when Independent Producers Marketing Corp. v. Cobb described the statute's purpose as stopping oil producers from retaining other people's money for their own use. There are no exceptions in the act, and the court has said so: equity is not a factor, because the statute provides none.

Other states price the dispute differently, and the spread is a map of how seriously each legislature takes delay. Oklahoma, under a 2026 amendment effective November 1, 2026, charges 15 percent a year on late proceeds — up from 12 percent compounded annually — but drops to the prime rate, capped at 6 percent, where the title is unmarketable. Texas charges two percentage points above the New York Federal Reserve rate on loans to depository institutions, and no interest at all while payment is properly suspended for a title dispute. West Virginia, for horizontal wells, charges the prime rate plus two percent, compounded quarterly. The same late check costs 18 percent in Wyoming, 15 percent in Oklahoma, and the prime rate plus two in West Virginia — and in Texas, if the suspension was proper, it costs nothing at all.

On top of the interest, Wyoming adds a reporting penalty that most owners never hear about: $100 a month for every month the operator fails to provide complete royalty information, under Section 30-5-303(c). And the litigation cost sits outside the statute entirely. The party that sues to release escrowed funds pays its own lawyers unless the court awards fees, and the escrow agent — the bank — charges its fees against the account whether the owner wins or loses. Virginia's coalbed methane rules state the deduction plainly: the Board orders the escrowed principal and accrued interest paid out "less escrow account fees." The owner who wins the title fight still pays the bank for holding the money during it.

The other side: why the payer should want the account

There is a case for the escrow, and it belongs to the operator as much as to the owner. The account is the operator's defense. An operator that deposits disputed proceeds into escrow on time has complied with the statute; an operator that holds the money on its own books has not, and the 18 percent interest is the price of the difference. The escrow agreement also caps the operator's exposure: once the money is with the bank, the operator is out of the dispute, and the heirs fight each other rather than the company. The fees are, from the operator's side, insurance — cheaper than the interest, cheaper than the lawsuit, cheaper than the headline of a court finding that the company kept somebody else's money.

That logic is sound, and it is also the reason the payer rarely feels the cost. The fees come out of the escrowed funds, not the operator's pocket, so the operator has little reason to negotiate them down. The royalty owner, who does pay them, was never a party to the negotiation. The result is a market where the person who chooses the agent does not pay the fee, and the person who pays the fee does not choose the agent. Any market structured that way will price high.

The practical advice follows from the structure. Before signing a division order, ask what the minimum pay amount is and what happens to funds below it. When a suspension notice arrives, ask for the escrow agreement itself — the Wyoming form is state-approved, the bank is named in it, and the fee schedule is in the agreement even if it is nowhere else. Ask whether the interest on the escrowed funds is the highest rate the bank offers, as Wyoming requires, or something lower. And count the dispute in interest, not in fees: on a $50,000 suspense balance, eighteen months at Wyoming's 18 percent is $13,500, and that number dwarfs every fee the bank will ever charge. The escrow is not expensive because of what the bank takes. It is expensive because of what the delay costs — and the delay is the one thing nobody in the room is paid to shorten.
