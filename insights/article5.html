How Long Royalty Escrow Takes to Release — and What Holds the Money

Wyoming gives an operator six months to pay and 30 days to release escrow once title is settled. The wait in between belongs to one document: the division order.

By Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026 · 7-minute read

The check arrived on a Tuesday, fourteen months after the well started producing. The owner had called the operator four times. The first three times, the answer was the same: the division order was still being verified. The fourth time, the answer changed — the order had cleared, the funds were released, and the check was in the mail. The owner did the arithmetic on the drive to the bank. Fourteen months of production, one phone call that mattered, and thirteen months in which nothing visible happened at all. The money had been there the whole time. The release had been waiting on a piece of paper.

Royalty escrow releases on a timetable that is mostly not a timetable. The statutes set the deadlines for the first payment with precision — six months after first sale in Wyoming and Oklahoma, 120 days after the end of the month of first sale in Texas — and then the process dissolves into verification, title clearance, and holdbacks, each of which runs on its own clock. The owner who asks "how long" gets an answer measured in months, and the honest version of that answer is that the release takes as long as the slowest document.

The statutory clock: precise, and only the beginning

Wyoming's Royalty Payment Act sets the frame. Proceeds from a first sale must be paid no later than six months after the first day of the month following that sale, and proceeds from later sales no later than 60 days after the end of the calendar month in which the sale occurred. Texas gives the operator 120 days after the end of the month of first sale, then 60 days after the end of the month for oil and 90 days for gas, unless the lease sets other timing. Oklahoma requires payment commencing not later than six months after the date of first sale, and thereafter not later than the last day of the second succeeding month after the end of the month of sale — the third succeeding month for gas production remitted to the operator. West Virginia, for horizontal wells, requires payment within 120 days, then 60 days for each later sale.

Those deadlines are the clock for payment when everything is in order. They are not the clock for release when it is not. Every one of those statutes suspends the deadline when payment cannot be made — for a title defect, a legal dispute, or a missing or unlocatable owner — and the suspension has no deadline of its own. The money sits until the reason for the suspension is cured, and the cure is a document, a court order, or a person being found. In Wyoming, once the title is finally determined, the escrow agent must pay all escrowed proceeds and accrued interest to the proper payee within 30 days of that final legal determination. Thirty days is the only release deadline in the statute, and it starts only when the dispute is over.

The division order: the document that most often delays payment

The document that most often delays payment is the division order. It is a short form — the owner's name, the fractional or decimal interest, the type of interest, the certification of title, and an agreement to notify the payor at least one month in advance of any change in the interest owned — and it is the operator's proof that it is paying the right person the right share. Until it is signed and verified, the operator will not pay, and the operator is entitled not to pay: Texas law expressly permits withholding without interest where the owner has not supplied the required title information, and the statutory division-order terms are the mechanism by which that withholding is documented.

The delay is rarely the form itself. It is everything the form depends on. A division order cannot be verified until the title is clear, and the title is not clear until the chain of ownership has been traced through every conveyance, probate, and assignment since the lease was signed. An heirship gap — a mineral interest that passed by inheritance without a probate, or through a probate in a county the operator has not searched — stops the verification cold. A missing tax identification number stops it. An address the operator cannot confirm stops it. Vitruvian Exploration, an operator whose owner-relations page lists the suspension reasons in plain language, names six: a missing tax ID number, an insufficient address, an assignment of interest, a notice of death, a lack of sufficient documentation or title confirming ownership, and disputes over proper ownership. Any one of them holds the money. The division order is the document that records the cure, and it is the last thing signed, not the first.

Title clearance: the long middle of the wait

Title clearance is the step with no statutory clock and the widest range. A clean title — a single owner, a recorded deed, a current address — clears in weeks, and the first check follows inside the statutory window. A title with a gap can take months or years. Oklahoma's statute anticipates the shape of the delay: where title is unmarketable, the interest rate drops to the prime rate, and after 120 days of unmarketability, either side may require the holder to interplead the proceeds into court for a determination of who is entitled to them. The interpleader is the law's admission that some titles will not clear on their own, and that the money needs a judge rather than a landman.

The holdback is the quieter cousin of the suspension. Even when the title is clear, an operator may hold back a portion of the proceeds against adjustments — overpayments, underpayments, tax deductions, and the accounting corrections that run years behind the production. The Government Accountability Office, in its November 2025 report on federal royalties, found net adjustments to federal royalty payments over fiscal 2014–2024 of negative $2.7 billion, about 2.8 percent of what was paid, and $300 million of adjustments that reached back to payments made four to six years earlier. The Office of Natural Resources Revenue approved $352 million in refunds and took up to seven years to verify accuracy. When the accounting itself runs years behind the production, the holdback is not a delay tactic. It is the system acknowledging that it does not yet know what it owes.

What the owner can actually move

The owner controls less of the timeline than the statutes suggest, and more than the silence suggests. The fastest lever is the division order itself: sign it, return it with the tax identification number, and confirm the mailing address in the same envelope. The second is the title package — the deed, the probate decree, the affidavit of death and heirship — sent before it is asked for, because the operator will not ask until the verification has already stalled. Oklahoma's statute names the affidavit of death and heirship as the document that cures an unmarketable title, and the holder who receives an acceptable one must pay. The third is the unclaimed-property search. In Wyoming, after three years with no owner contact, the operator must turn the proceeds over to the State Treasurer, where they are held indefinitely with no fee to claim — and the owner who finds the money there has also found the reason the checks stopped.

There is a counterargument to the impatience, and it is worth hearing. The verification exists because the alternative is worse: an operator that pays the wrong heir cannot get the money back, and the rightful owner who was paid late at least gets paid. The 30-day release rule in Wyoming, the interpleader in Oklahoma, and the interest penalties in every producing state are all built on the same premise — that the money should wait for the right owner rather than go to the wrong one quickly. The system is slow because it is careful, and careful is a virtue when the asset is a family's mineral interest.

But careful has a cost, and the cost falls on the owner who did everything right. The heir who signed the division order, supplied the tax number, and confirmed the address still waits while the operator verifies the cousin who did none of those things. The money is released when the last document arrives, and the last document belongs to the slowest party. Fourteen months, one phone call, and a check on a Tuesday: that is not a failure of the system. It is the system working as designed, and the design assumes the owner will wait.
