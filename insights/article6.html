The Suspended Royalty: When Escrow Holds the Money — and How Owners Get It Back

A suspended royalty is not lost — it is held in escrow, in suspense, or by the state. Getting it back means curing the title defect, the missing heir, or the dispute, in that order.

By Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026 · 9-minute read

The envelope from the operator was thin, and the news inside was thinner: your royalty payments have been suspended. No check. No date for the next one. A phone number for the division order department and a sentence about title. The owner who opens that letter is usually not a lawyer, not an heir to a fortune, and not prepared for what follows — which is a process with a name, a set of steps, and a cost for each one, none of which the letter explains. The money is not gone. It is held. Getting it back is a matter of knowing why it was held and in what order to fix it.

Suspension is the ordinary state of a disputed royalty. It is not a punishment, and it is not a seizure. It is what an operator does when it cannot tell, with the certainty its lawyers require, who owns the interest — and the law in most producing states requires it. The question is never whether the money exists. The operator's own records say it does. The question is who it belongs to, and the answer is a paper trail.

Why the checks stop

The reasons fall into a short list, and one operator's owner-relations page states them with unusual candor. Vitruvian Exploration lists six: a missing tax identification number, an insufficient address, an assignment of interest, a notice of death, a lack of sufficient documentation or title confirming ownership, and disputes involving proper ownership of the interests. Behind those six sit the three causes that account for most suspensions: a title defect, a missing heir, and a dispute between claimants.

A title defect is a gap in the chain of ownership — a conveyance that was never recorded, a probate that was never opened, a mineral interest that passed by inheritance without a decree. A missing heir is the same gap with a person attached: the interest belongs to someone the operator cannot find, or to heirs the operator cannot identify, and the payments accumulate in suspense until the heir appears or the state takes the money. A dispute is two people claiming the same interest — siblings over a deed, a surface owner against a mineral owner, a coal claimant against a gas claimant — and the operator, faced with two claimants, pays neither.

The statutes sort these causes into two buckets, and the bucket decides what happens to the money. In Wyoming, when payment cannot be made for any reason within the statutory time, the operator must deposit the proceeds into an escrow account in a federally insured Wyoming bank, under a standard escrow form approved by the Attorney General, earning the highest rate the institution offers. In West Virginia, a holder must report and remit amounts reserved for unknown or unlocatable owners to the State Treasurer each calendar quarter, and in a commission-ordered horizontal well unit the operator must deposit the money into an escrow account bearing a market rate of interest. In Virginia, the Gas and Oil Board holds escrow accounts with tract subaccounts for pooled units, and orders the money paid out — principal and accrued interest, less escrow fees — within 30 days of a court decision, an arbitration determination, or an agreement among all claimants. The money is held by a bank, by the state, or by the operator's suspense ledger, but in every case it is held, not kept.

The steps to get it back

The first step costs nothing and takes a phone call: find out which bucket the money is in. Ask the operator whether the interest is in suspense on its books, in a bank escrow account, or remitted to the state as unclaimed property — and ask for the reason in writing. The answer determines everything that follows. A missing tax number is cured with a form. An insufficient address is cured with a letter. A title defect is cured with documents. A dispute is cured with a court or an agreement. The owner who does not know the reason cannot fix it, and the operator is not obliged to volunteer the diagnosis.

The second step is the paper. For a missing heir, the document is usually an affidavit of death and heirship — the sworn statement, filed in the county, that names the decedent, the heirs, and their shares. Oklahoma's statute names it explicitly as the document that cures an unmarketable title, and the holder who receives an acceptable affidavit must pay. For a probate gap, the document is the decree itself, or the opening of a probate that was never opened — a process that takes months and costs a filing fee and a lawyer's time, and that is the most expensive step in the ordinary case. For a title defect, the document is the missing conveyance: a deed, a correction deed, or a quiet-title action, which is the most expensive step of all, because it asks a court to declare who owns the interest. The time and cost run in that order: a form is days and free; an affidavit is weeks and a notary; a probate is months and thousands of dollars; a quiet-title action is a year or more and the price of litigation.

The third step is the division order. Once the title is cured, the owner signs the order — the name, the fractional interest, the certification of title, the agreement to give a month's notice of any change — and the operator verifies it against the cured title. This is the step that most often delays payment even after the underlying problem is fixed, because the verification is the operator's, not the owner's, and it runs on the operator's schedule. The payments, when they resume, are retroactive to the date of suspension: Vitruvian states it plainly, and the principle holds across operators — the money that accumulated while the interest was suspended is paid, with the interest the escrow earned, once the cure is accepted.

The fourth step, for the owner who cannot find the money at all, is the state. In Wyoming, after three years with no owner contact, the operator must turn the proceeds over to the State Treasurer, where they are held indefinitely and can be claimed for free — no fee, no deadline, but proof of ownership or heirship required. The search is free, through the Treasurer's unclaimed-property program and the national MissingMoney.com database, and it is the step the heirs most often skip: the money is not with the operator any more, and the operator's records will say so, if the heir asks the right question.

What it costs, and who pays

The cost of getting suspended royalties back is the cost of the cure, and it falls entirely on the owner. A missing tax number costs nothing. An affidavit of death and heirship costs a lawyer's hour and a filing fee. A probate costs thousands. A quiet-title action costs the price of a lawsuit, and the operator does not share it — the escrow agent's fees, in Virginia's formulation, come out of the escrowed funds themselves, "less escrow account fees," so the owner pays the bank for holding the money during the dispute the owner did not start. Wyoming adds a small lever the other way: $100 a month for every month the operator fails to provide complete royalty information. It is a small number against a large suspension, but it is the statute's way of saying the operator's silence has a price too.

The interest is the part of the story that cuts in the owner's favor, and it is also the part that is most often misunderstood. In Wyoming, the 18 percent interest runs on late payment — but the escrow deposit is the operator's compliance, and once the money is in escrow earning the bank's highest rate, the 18 percent clock is not running against the operator for the period the money was properly escrowed. In Oklahoma, where title is unmarketable, the rate drops to the prime rate, capped at 6 percent from November 1, 2026, and no interest runs while a probate needed to cure title is pending until the payor receives the final decree. In Texas, no interest is owed on payments withheld over a title dispute or an unmet title requirement. The owner who expects 18 percent on a properly suspended royalty will be disappointed. The owner who expects the bank's rate, and the principal, and the retroactive payment, will get all three.

The case for the suspension — and its limit

The suspension exists for a reason that survives scrutiny. An operator that pays the wrong heir cannot recover the money, and the rightful owner who was paid late at least gets paid. The escrow, the suspense ledger, and the unclaimed-property program are all built on the premise that the money should wait for the right owner rather than go to the wrong one quickly. The Wyoming Supreme Court has enforced that premise since 1986, when it described the Royalty Payment Act's purpose as stopping oil producers from retaining other people's money for their own use, and again in 2025, when it affirmed a violation in Chesapeake Exploration v. Morton Production Co. where disputed proceeds were withheld without being placed in escrow.

The limit of the system is the owner who never learns the money was suspended. The rule protects the owners who know enough to call, to file the affidavit, to search the unclaimed-property portal. The ones who do not know — the heir who moved, the widow who never saw a division order, the family that did not know the mineral interest existed — are still waiting on a check that was never sent, in a system where the accounting runs years behind the production. The Government Accountability Office found $300 million in adjustments to federal royalty payments that reached back four to six years, and the Office of Natural Resources Revenue took up to seven years to verify accuracy. The money is held, and it is held indefinitely in Wyoming's unclaimed-property program, but it is not delivered. It waits for someone to come and claim it.

That is the final step, and the only one that matters: ask. Ask the operator for the suspension reason in writing. Ask whether the funds are in escrow, in suspense, or with the state. Search the Treasurer's program and MissingMoney.com under every spelling of the family name. File the affidavit, or open the probate, or sign the division order — in that order, and as early as possible, because the cure is the clock. The money is not lost. It is held by a bank, or by the state, or on the operator's books, earning interest, waiting for the paper that proves whose it is. The paper is the owner's job. Everything else is already done.
